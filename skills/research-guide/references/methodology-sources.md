# Methodological Sources and Their Scope

The research workflow is a synthesis. These public sources motivate particular practices; they do not establish that the complete workflow guarantees novelty, correctness, or research success. Consult field-specific methods when applying the workflow to an actual study.

## Question importance and a credible approach

Richard W. Hamming, *You and Your Research*, Bell Communications Research Colloquium, 7 March 1986. [Public transcript hosted by the University of Virginia](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.pdf).

Hamming connects choosing an important problem with having a credible approach to it. He also discusses reframing isolated tasks into problems whose solutions others can build upon. This supports asking both why a question matters and what makes progress possible.

Scope: a reflective talk based on personal experience, not an empirical evaluation of a selection algorithm. It does not justify ranking topics by prestige, promising major discoveries, or dismissing important questions merely because they are currently difficult.

## Competing explanations and discriminating evidence

John R. Platt, *Strong Inference*, *Science* 146(3642), 347-353 (1964). DOI: [10.1126/science.146.3642.347](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.146.3642.347). [Public text hosted by the University of Wisconsin](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/science64_strong_inference.pdf).

Platt describes an iterative approach: formulate alternatives, devise experiments whose outcomes distinguish them, and refine the remaining possibilities after observing the result. This motivates an explicit connection between a question, predictions, and the next decision.

Scope: a methodological argument, not a guarantee that every question admits a decisive experiment. Exploratory, observational, mathematical, and descriptive work may need different evidence forms. The hosted text is a transcription from microfilm and notes uncertainty about some punctuation; use paraphrase rather than relying on exact quotation.

## Feasibility before larger commitments

National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, [*Pilot Studies: Common Uses and Misuses*](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/grants/pilot-studies-common-uses-and-misuses).

The guidance separates feasibility and acceptability questions from definitive efficacy questions. It recommends context-specific feasibility benchmarks and cautions against treating unstable pilot effect estimates as a sufficient basis for a larger efficacy study's sample size.

Scope: clinical intervention feasibility studies. The transferable distinction is between establishing that a proposed study can work and establishing its final scientific claim. Its specific restrictions should not become universal prohibitions on hypothesis testing or statistical inference in every kind of scientific pilot.

## Reproducibility and replication

National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science* (2019). DOI: [10.17226/25303](https://doi.org/10.17226/25303). [Chapter 3: Understanding Reproducibility and Replicability](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25303/chapter/6).

The report distinguishes computational reproducibility using the same data and procedures from replication using independently obtained data to address the same scientific question. It treats consistency in relation to uncertainty and study conditions.

Scope: the report explicitly recognizes differences in disciplinary terminology. State which definitions are being used. A rerunnable computation alone does not establish a valid measurement, a sound model, causal identification, or generality beyond the studied conditions.

## Measurement uncertainty

National Institute of Standards and Technology, *Technical Note 1297: Guidelines for Evaluating and Expressing the Uncertainty of NIST Measurement Results*, [Section 2: Classification of Components of Uncertainty](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-2-classification-components-uncertainty).

The guidance treats uncertainty as part of a measurement result and distinguishes components evaluated statistically from those evaluated by other means. It also addresses correlations among uncertainty components and cautions against equating these evaluation categories with random and systematic effects.

Scope: measurement science. It does not provide a universal numerical measure for uncertainty about an explanation, causal assumption, or research direction. Use it where a quantitative measurement model is appropriate.

## How to use these references

Cite the specific source when explaining a borrowed principle. Keep recommendations distinguishable from established findings. Verify current field-specific standards and literature when designing a real project; these foundational references do not establish that a proposed research gap remains open.
